# 2. 💵 Why do I keep mentioning the 10Y?

**10Y = U.S. 10-year Treasury yield.**

And you're right to challenge me on this.

### The important point:

**The 10Y is NOT similar to the VIX.**

They measure completely different things.

**VIX → expected equity-market volatility.**
**10Y yield → the market's required interest rate for lending to the U.S. government for ~10 years.**

But the 10Y matters to you because it can influence **how aggressively traders value and buy stocks**, particularly growth/technology stocks.

Think of it like this:

> **VIX tells you how scared/excited the stock market is.**
> **10Y tells you how expensive money is becoming.**

### Why does that matter to your trading?

Suppose you have:

**NVDA → $200**

and the market suddenly decides that Treasury yields are going substantially higher.

Investors may become less willing to pay a high multiple for NVDA.

That can cause:

**10Y ↑ → growth-stock valuation pressure → NVDA/AMD/etc. ↓**

You don't necessarily care about the Treasury itself.

You care because **it can become a macro force pushing the stocks you trade.**

### For your style

You are primarily:

**long equities + technical + short/medium holding periods + harvesting profits + cash available**

So I'd interpret the 10Y like this:

**10Y falling sharply**
→ 🟢 generally favorable for growth/tech
→ potentially more buyers
→ easier environment for your long trades.

**10Y rising sharply**
→ 🔴 generally unfavorable
→ particularly for growth/tech
→ more difficult long trades.

But here's the important nuance:

**A slowly rising 10Y isn't necessarily bad.**

You care much more about **large, rapid moves and breaks of important levels**.

That's why I keep mentioning **5%**. It's not because 5% is some magical number. It's because a rapid move through a psychologically and historically important level can alter market behavior.

# 3. 📉 What is a "washout"?

A **washout** is basically:

> **A violent bout of selling that forces weak holders out of a stock.**

Imagine:

NVDA:

$200 → $190 → $180 → $165

People panic.

Some sell because their technical stops trigger.

Others sell because they don't want to lose more.

Short sellers pile in.

Eventually most of the weak buyers have disappeared.

Then:

**$165 → $172 → $180 → $190**

That's the interesting part.

The washout can create a **technical reset**.

### Why can it be GOOD for you?

Because you're a buyer of weakness.

That's actually very important.

Your natural trading behavior has historically been something like:

> "It went down. I can buy some. If it goes down further, I can buy more."

A washout can therefore create exactly the kind of **deep discount / high-volatility opportunity** that attracts your style.

But there's a trap.

### Washout ≠ bottom.

A stock can:

**$200 → $170 washout → $140 → $110**

So buying during a washout can be excellent **if the selling exhausts itself**.

It can be disastrous if you interpret **"very cheap" as "finished going down."**

For you:

**Controlled pullback → 🟢 potentially attractive**

**Capitulation → 🟡/🟢 potentially attractive, but dangerous**

**Fundamental deterioration + continued selling → 🔴 dangerous**

# 4. 📈 What are growth stocks?

A **growth stock** is a company where investors expect relatively high future growth in things such as:

* revenue
* earnings
* market share
* cash flow

Typical examples include:

**NVDA, AMD, PLTR, CRWD, META, AMZN, MSFT**

Many technology companies fall into this category.

### What other types exist?

There isn't one universal classification system, but traders commonly talk about:

**Growth**
High expected growth.

**Value**
Stocks considered inexpensive relative to earnings/assets/cash flow.

**Income / dividend**
Companies where dividends are an important part of the return.

**Defensive**
Businesses whose demand tends to be relatively stable during economic stress.

**Cyclical**
Companies whose business changes substantially with the economic cycle.

**Small-cap**
Smaller companies, often with greater volatility.

**Large-cap / mega-cap**
Large, established companies.

**Momentum**
Stocks moving strongly in one direction, regardless of whether the underlying classification is growth/value/etc.

**Speculative**
High uncertainty and potentially extreme price movements.

And importantly:

### A stock can belong to several categories simultaneously.

NVDA could be:

**large-cap + growth + technology + momentum + semiconductor**

That's much more useful than trying to put it into one box.

# 5. 🏦 What's a "hawkish Fed"?

Very simply:

### 🦅 Hawkish Fed

A Fed that is more concerned about **inflation** and therefore more inclined toward:

* higher interest rates
* keeping rates high longer
* reducing monetary stimulus
* resisting premature rate cuts

The opposite is:

### 🕊️ Dovish Fed

More concerned about:

* economic weakness
* unemployment
* recession risk

and therefore more inclined toward:

* lower rates
* rate cuts
* easier financial conditions.

There are also situations that don't fit neatly into either:

### ⚖️ Neutral / balanced Fed

Basically:

> "We're watching the data and aren't committing strongly either way."

And:

### 🔄 Data-dependent Fed

Similar idea, but emphasizing that future decisions depend heavily on incoming economic data.

## Which Fed environment is best for YOUR trading?

I'd separate **market environment** from "good/bad Fed."

For your long-equity trading:

### 🟢 Most favorable

**Dovish / easing Fed + falling yields + improving breadth**

That tends to create a favorable environment for long growth/technology trades.

### 🟡 Potentially favorable

**Neutral/data-dependent Fed + stable yields + healthy market**

This can actually be excellent for harvesting because stocks can trend without enormous macro shocks.

### 🟠 More difficult

**Hawkish Fed + rapidly rising yields**

Particularly painful for high-multiple growth stocks.

### 🔴 Most difficult

**Hawkish Fed + rising yields + falling equities + deteriorating breadth**

That's the combination I really care about for you.

And notice something important:

**I don't actually care very much whether the Fed is philosophically hawkish or dovish.**

I care about:

> **What is the market doing in response?**

If the Fed says something hawkish and NVDA goes **+4%**, that's much more interesting to you than the adjective "hawkish."

# 6. 🧩 "Liquid laggard" — what does that bizarre phrase mean?

😂 This is actually a good question.

It's two independent adjectives/nouns:

### **Liquid**

Means:

> **A stock that can be bought/sold in substantial quantity without moving its price excessively.**

Think:

**NVDA, AAPL, MSFT**

rather than some obscure microcap with 20,000 shares traded per day.

### **Laggard**

Means:

> **A stock performing relatively poorly compared with its peers or the broader market.**

So:

> **Liquid laggard = a highly tradable stock that is currently underperforming.**

Likewise:

### **Liquid leader**

= highly tradable stock currently outperforming.

That's why I use the expression in the Market Risk Watch.

# 7. 🧠 There are MANY combinations

Here's the useful vocabulary for you:

### **Liquid leader**

High trading liquidity + strong relative performance.

🟢 **Potentially excellent hunting ground for you.**

---

### **Liquid laggard**

High liquidity + weak relative performance.

🟡/🟢 **Potentially interesting**, because you're comfortable buying weakness.

But you need to distinguish:

**temporary weakness** from **structural deterioration**.

---

### **Illiquid leader**

Strong price movement but poor liquidity.

🟠 Interesting but dangerous.

You can make money quickly—and have trouble getting out quickly.

---

### **Illiquid laggard**

Weak + difficult to trade.

🔴 Usually unattractive for your style.

---

### **Momentum leader**

Stock with strong directional price momentum.

🟢 Potentially very attractive.

This is especially relevant if you're trying to transition from:

**"buy weakness / harvest quickly"**

toward:

**"let winners run."**

---

### **Relative-strength leader**

Outperforming its sector/index.

🟢 **Very interesting for you.**

Example:

NASDAQ −1%

PANW +2%

That is meaningful relative strength.

---

### **Relative-strength laggard**

Underperforming its benchmark/peers.

🟡 Potential opportunity **if** the weakness is temporary.

---

### **Oversold stock**

Has experienced unusually strong recent selling.

🟢/🟡 Potential rebound candidate.

But:

> **Oversold does NOT mean undervalued or finished falling.**

---

### **Overbought stock**

Has risen unusually rapidly.

🟡 Potential pullback candidate.

But:

> **Overbought does NOT mean "short it."**

Strong stocks can remain overbought for a long time.

---

### **High-beta stock**

Moves more aggressively than the market.

🟢 **Potentially excellent for your harvesting style.**

But also 🔴 higher risk.

---

### **Low-beta stock**

Moves less aggressively than the market.

🟡 Usually less interesting for your style unless you're specifically seeking stability.
